The Twilight of the Parking Orbit: Why Geostationary Communications Satellites Are Becoming Obsolete

GaianSpace 2026-08-13 9 min read

For more than sixty years, the communications satellite has meant one thing: a large spacecraft parked 36,000 kilometers above the equator, holding still in the sky so a single dish on Earth could stare at it forever. That architecture, geostationary Earth orbit, or GEO, built the modern satellite industry. It carried telephone trunks across oceans, relayed military command links, and beamed television into hundreds of millions of homes. It is now being displaced, not by a better GEO satellite, but by something that looks almost like the opposite idea: thousands of smaller spacecraft racing through low Earth orbit, handing a user from one bird to the next every few minutes. The classic GEO communications relay is becoming obsolete. Broadcast is following more slowly, but it is following. The evidence is no longer theoretical. It is in the latency numbers, the subscriber counts, the canceled GEO orders, and the wave of new constellations that came online over the last eighteen months.

The Architecture That Won, Until It Didn't

GEO is an elegant trick of orbital mechanics. At roughly 35,786 kilometers above the equator, a satellite's orbital period matches Earth's rotation, so it appears fixed in the sky [1]. One spacecraft can see about a third of the planet. Three well-placed satellites can cover the inhabited Earth. Ground terminals can be simple, because they never have to track a moving target. For analog television, one-way video, and thin voice circuits, that combination of coverage and simplicity was unbeatable.

The price of that elegance is distance. Light itself takes about 120 milliseconds to climb to GEO and another 120 to come back. Add processing, encoding, and the hop through a gateway, and a typical GEO internet session sits between 500 and 800 milliseconds of round-trip latency [2][3]. That is not a software problem and it is not a "next generation payload" problem. It is a geometry problem. No amount of extra transponders on a bigger bus can shrink the speed of light.

Low Earth orbit sits between about 160 and 2,000 kilometers, with most broadband constellations clustered around 340 to 570 kilometers, roughly one-sixtieth the altitude of GEO [4][5]. The same photon that spent a quarter of a second commuting to a parked satellite now makes the round trip in a few milliseconds. Measured user latency on Starlink has fallen to a median of about 45 milliseconds in the United States, overlapping terrestrial cable and 4G rather than competing with it [6][7]. That is the physical reason the old relay model is losing: once you can put a mesh of satellites close to Earth, the parked high bird stops being a clever shortcut and starts being a long way around.

The Numbers That Ended the Debate

Independent speed tests have made the comparison uncomfortable for GEO broadband operators. Ookla's 2025 measurements of U.S. users showed Starlink at a median download of about 105 Mbps and a median upload of about 15 Mbps, against HughesNet at 48 / 4.4 Mbps and Viasat at 49 / 1.1 Mbps. Latency told a harsher story still: 45 milliseconds for Starlink, 683 for HughesNet, 684 for Viasat [6][7]. HughesNet and Viasat both improved download speeds over three years. It was not enough. HughesNet's subscriber base fell nearly 29 percent from 1.22 million in 2022 to 853,000 in early 2025. Viasat's consumer count collapsed almost 68 percent, from 590,000 to 189,000, over a slightly longer window [7].

Maritime and mobility customers, once locked into GEO because there was no alternative at sea or in the air, are making the same switch. A sailor can carry a LEO terminal under one arm. The GEO antenna it replaces typically delivers around 20 Mbps at roughly 800 milliseconds. The LEO service delivers 100 to 200 Mbps at about 50 milliseconds [8]. For video calls, cloud software, VoIP, and anything that expects the internet to behave like the internet, that is not an incremental upgrade. It is a different product.

Think of GEO as a single lighthouse on a distant cliff, bright and reliable if you are willing to wait for the beam. LEO is a swarm of harbor lights moving along the coast. You never stare at one lamp for long, but you are never far from the next one, and the message arrives before you have finished forming it.

A Wave of Constellations, Not a Single Competitor

It would be a mistake to treat this as a Starlink story that other operators might still outwait. Starlink is simply the first constellation to reach industrial scale. It began 2026 with roughly 9,500 working satellites, later crossing the 10,000 mark, with U.S. approval for thousands more and a long-stated destination near 42,000 [9][10][11]. Its customer base nearly doubled in a year to about 9.2 million users across some 155 countries by early 2026, and crossed 10 million shortly afterward [9][12].

The rest of the sky is filling in behind it. Amazon rebranded Project Kuiper as Amazon Leo and spent 2026 ramping launches toward a first-generation fleet of 3,236 satellites, with a larger generation planned beyond that. It began offering enterprise service and advertised consumer-class speeds of up to 1 Gbps down and 400 Mbps up on its high-end terminal, with private paths into AWS [9][10]. Eutelsat's OneWeb, already a completed first-generation fleet of roughly 600 satellites, saw LEO service revenue jump 60 percent in a half year and is ordering hundreds of replenishment spacecraft plus Europe's sovereign IRIS² follow-on [9][11]. Telesat Lightspeed entered 2026 with more than a billion dollars of backlog two years before service, aimed at enterprise and government rather than the consumer mass market [9]. China is building two national systems, Qianfan (Thousand Sails) and Guowang, that together target on the order of 28,000 satellites. Blue Origin has disclosed TeraWave, a 5,408-satellite LEO/MEO design aimed at fiber-like links for a small number of high-end users [9][11].

Novaspace, the market-intelligence firm formed from Euroconsult, now describes the capacity shift in almost binary terms. Between 2023 and 2025, global satellite communications supply more than tripled, to about 105 terabits per second. Ninety-five percent of that supply is non-geostationary. Ninety-three percent is Starlink alone. Novaspace expects non-geostationary systems to provide more than 99 percent of new capacity added through 2030, and to account for over 95 percent of global satcom and connectivity supply after 2026 [13][9]. When nearly all new bits in the sky are coming from LEO, the parked fleet is no longer the industry. It is the installed base.

The Communications Relay Is Already Losing Its Customers

The original GEO communications satellite was a bent-pipe relay: a microwave mirror in the sky. A signal went up from a gateway, came down to a user, and that was the product. That product is the first to go obsolete, because every assumption it was built on has inverted.

  • Latency is no longer optional. Video calls, remote desktops, financial sessions, and cloud applications assume a terrestrial-like round trip. A 600-millisecond GEO hop makes those applications feel broken. A 40-millisecond LEO hop does not [2][6][7].
  • Capacity per user now comes from numbers, not from one giant bus. A single high-throughput GEO satellite is still a remarkable machine, but a constellation can reuse spectrum over thousands of small footprints and add capacity by launching another batch. The industry's new supply is already 95 percent non-geostationary [13].
  • The terminal got smaller than the use case. GEO broadband still wants a carefully installed dish. LEO terminals have shrunk to something a vehicle, a small boat, or a disaster team can deploy in minutes [8]. Direct-to-device services now reach ordinary phones for messaging and, increasingly, data, a market GEO relays were never designed to serve [7][12].
  • Resilience favors the mesh. One GEO failure, or one jammed uplink, can take a region offline. In a constellation, a lost satellite is a hole the network routes around. Governments that once bought GEO capacity as strategic infrastructure are now shopping for sovereign or allied LEO access instead [9].

Eutelsat, which still operates a large GEO fleet, has said the quiet part out loud. "LEO is the only part of the connectivity market that is really growing," chief communications officer Joanna Darlington told Via Satellite in early 2026. "What we see in day-to-day operations is that LEO is massively outpacing demand for GEO. And in many cases, it's starting to cannibalize it as well" [9]. That is not an analyst's forecast. It is an incumbent describing its own order book.

Broadcast Is the Slower Casualty, Not the Exception

If any GEO mission still looks safe, it is broadcast. One satellite can paint an entire continent with the same television multiplex. Millions of cheap, fixed dishes can receive it at once. No constellation of moving LEO satellites is as naturally suited to "one-to-many" live video, and live sports still need a pipe that does not care how many people tune in at the same second.

That advantage is real, and it is shrinking. The threat is not only LEO. It is the combination of fiber, 5G, and streaming services that have trained audiences to pull video on demand rather than receive a scheduled multiplex. Novaspace projects that data applications, 21 percent of satellite service revenues in 2024, will overtake video by 2033 and reach 55 percent by 2034. All of the industry's expected service-revenue growth through 2034 comes from data. Video is the shrinking half of the ledger [13]. Satellite television still books tens of billions of dollars, but it is a drag on growth: the sector's 2024 video revenue fell about 6 percent, and operators in North America and Western Europe lost roughly 4.8 million net satellite pay-TV subscribers in 2025 as global streaming subscriptions passed a billion [14][15].

The largest remaining GEO operators are already behaving as if broadcast is a cash cow to be milked, not a franchise to expand. SES closed its acquisition of Intelsat in July 2025 and then canceled two software-defined GEO satellites, IS-41 and IS-44, that Intelsat had ordered in 2022. Eutelsat had already scrapped its Flexsat Americas GEO program. SES's media business is still profitable and still signs multi-year renewals, but it is no longer the company's center of gravity. In the first half of 2026, connectivity overtook broadcast inside SES for the first time, 64 percent of revenue against 36 percent [16][17][18]. Like-for-like media revenue continues to decline as standard-definition channels are switched off and mature markets optimize away spare capacity [17].

In other words, GEO broadcast is not going to vanish next Tuesday. It is becoming a runoff business: useful for live events, residual direct-to-home markets, and contribution links, but no longer the reason to keep ordering 15-year, multi-hundred-million-dollar spacecraft. When the last big operators stop replacing broadcast satellites one-for-one, the architecture will have ended even if a few aging birds are still feeding dishes.

The Incumbents Are Voting With Their Capital

Satellite operators reveal what they believe by what they buy. For most of the 2010s, the default growth move was another large GEO platform. That default is gone. Novaspace now describes a "wait and see" posture among traditional operators, fewer large GEO orders, and a preference for smaller, regionally targeted satellites if anything is ordered at all. Integrating non-geostationary capacity has become a survival strategy, not a side bet [13].

The 2025–2026 consolidation wave only makes that clearer. SES bought Intelsat and immediately began retiring overlapping GEO plans. Eutelsat folded OneWeb into its identity and is pouring on the order of two billion euros into LEO replenishment and IRIS². Telesat, once a classic GEO/video company, is staking its future on Lightspeed. Even where GEO fleets remain large, they are being treated as a depreciating asset to be rationalized across a multi-orbit story [9][16][18].

None of this requires claiming that every spacecraft at 36,000 kilometers will be switched off. Weather imagers, some missile-warning sensors, and a handful of sovereign communications payloads will stay in GEO because a fixed look-angle still has value for those missions [4][19]. The claim is narrower and stronger: the GEO satellite in its current commercial form, a large bent-pipe or high-throughput relay sold as the way to move bits, and increasingly the way to move television, is no longer the competitive architecture. It is a legacy layer. New demand is not going there.

What "Obsolete" Actually Means Here

Obsolete does not mean "physically impossible" or "already gone." Copper telephone lines are obsolete as a growth architecture and still carry calls. Mainframes are obsolete as a general-purpose computing model and still settle bank transactions. GEO communications satellites are entering that category.

Three tests are useful. First, would a new operator with a blank sheet of paper and a few billion dollars choose a handful of parked relays to sell broadband, backhaul, or mobility? The last decade of filings, launches, and customer wins says no. Second, are the existing GEO operators still funding the next generation of the same machines? They are canceling them [16][18]. Third, is the remaining GEO demand interactive communications or a shrinking broadcast/contribution remnant? The revenue mix has already flipped at the largest combined GEO operator on Earth [17].

There is a serious counter-argument, and it should be stated plainly. A February 2025 symposium in Aerospace America asked whether GEO satellites would become obsolete. The published answers were some version of "not entirely." The shared view was that GEO would lose importance, drift into a backup or niche role, and see less motivation for upgrades, while LEO became the preferred architecture for broadband [8]. That is not a defense of the current form. It is an epitaph written in cautious engineering language. A technology that the industry's own specialists expect to become a backup is a technology whose prime is over.

The remaining risks of the LEO transition, debris, spectrum crowding, and the capital intensity of replenishing five-year spacecraft, are real [19][20]. They do not restore GEO's old advantages. They are arguments for operating constellations more carefully, not for climbing back to 36,000 kilometers and accepting a half-second delay.

Conclusion: The Sky Moved Closer

Geostationary communications satellites did not fail. They completed the job they were designed for, covering a planet that had no fiber, no 5G, and no way to track a moving spacecraft with a cheap antenna. The world that needed them has been replaced by one that needs low latency, dense reuse of spectrum, small terminals, and networks that can lose a node without losing a region. Those requirements belong to low Earth orbit.

The communications relay version of GEO is already obsolete as a growth architecture. Broadcast, the last application that still fits the parked-satellite model, is a declining cash business rather than a reason to rebuild the fleet. New constellations from SpaceX, Amazon, Eutelsat, Telesat, China, Europe, and a lengthening list of specialists are not waiting for permission to finish the shift. They are already carrying the bits. The stationary stars will linger on the horizon for a while, the way old lighthouses do. The traffic has already moved into the harbor.
